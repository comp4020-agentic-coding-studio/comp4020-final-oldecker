# Verification as a condition of agentic autonomy

Good agentic software development requires deciding what evidence makes generated work trustworthy. For maintained applications with real users, I argue that autonomy should expand only alongside the capacity to verify its results. This does not mean a human must inspect every action. It means developers must design checks that can expose failure, rather than accepting an agent’s account of success. The important skill is deciding what those checks establish, where they remain incomplete, and when their cost is justified.

Research on automation helps explain why this matters. [Lee and See (2004)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1518/hfes.46.1.50_30392) argue for appropriate reliance: trust should correspond to an automated system’s capabilities and the circumstances of its use. [Parasuraman and Manzey (2010)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055) show why simply retaining human oversight is insufficient. Their review connects complacency and automation bias to attention, finding that expertise and instructions do not reliably eliminate these problems. Neither paper studies coding agents, so applying their findings here requires caution. Nevertheless, they challenge the assumption that a developer supervising several agents will necessarily notice when one produces a plausible but incorrect result.

Practitioner accounts suggest that verification can be built into the development environment. [Willison (2025)](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Oct/7/vibe-engineering/) argues that productive agentic development rewards automated testing, code review, manual quality assurance and preview environments. [Carlini’s (2026)](https://www.anthropic.com/research/building-c-compiler) account of building a compiler with parallel agents makes a similar point: much of his effort went into tests and feedback that allowed agents to progress autonomously. These are experience reports, not controlled demonstrations of general productivity gains. Their value is in identifying a practical mechanism: reliable feedback can make continued human intervention less necessary.

This also complicates what “independent verification” means. An agent executing a test produces stronger evidence than an agent saying its code works. However, a test written from the same mistaken interpretation as the implementation can pass while the requirement remains unmet. Independence therefore concerns the basis of the check, not merely which agent performs it. Developers still need to establish acceptance criteria and examine whether the checks meaningfully test them. In my own project, a build and a passing test suite were never treated as sufficient on their own: requiring tests to run twice against a scratch database, and a real browser check at each supported width, let me catch agent-reported successes that a green build alone would have missed.

The strongest objection is economic. Verification takes time, and an agent that generates work faster than it can be checked may simply move effort elsewhere. [Geng and Neubig (2026)](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.21489v2) make this trade-off concrete. Their CAID system combines isolated workspaces, coordinated integration and executable verification, improving performance on software-engineering benchmarks. Yet they report higher API costs and no substantial reduction in elapsed runtime. Because these mechanisms are bundled together, the study cannot establish that verification alone caused the improvement. It does show why parallel activity should not be equated with faster delivery.

My answer is not that verification always pays for itself. A workflow should be judged by the cost of reaching an acceptable result, including checking, integration and correction. Reusable automated checks may make that cost manageable across many changes; repeated manual inspection may not. If checking a particular agent’s output consistently costs more than completing the task directly, reducing delegation is a reasonable engineering decision. Autonomy is useful only when the resulting work justifies its total cost.

The claim also has limits. Disposable experiments can reasonably accept uncertainty that would be inappropriate in an application holding other people’s data. Verification cannot settle every question either: tests can establish access restrictions, but cannot prove that a social interface feels welcoming. Those judgments still require engagement with users. My position therefore concerns maintained applications with identifiable obligations, rather than every use of generated code. Evidence of consistently reliable outcomes with substantially lighter checking would justify relaxing the workflow. Until then, good agentic practice means making confidence answerable to evidence—and granting autonomy where that evidence is strong enough.
